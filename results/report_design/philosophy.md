# Instrument Record

*A design philosophy for documents that hold measurements.*

Instrument Record treats a page the way a field notebook treats a day of observation: as a calibrated surface on which
evidence is laid down, not decorated. Space is the primary instrument. Wide, quiet margins frame a single dominant
observation per page, and everything else sits at a respectful distance from it, small, exact and secondary. The page
should feel surveyed rather than filled, as if every coordinate was measured before anything was placed. Each composition
must read as meticulously crafted, the product of painstaking attention by someone who has spent a career laying out
evidence.

Colour is restricted to the materials of a laboratory: warm paper, graphite ink, and two signal colours used only where a
measurement demands them, one for the condition that failed and one for the condition that held. No colour appears for
ornament. A reader should be able to learn the whole palette in a glance and then trust it completely, because it never
changes its meaning from one page to the next. The calibration of these tones should look like the result of countless
quiet adjustments.

Typography works in three registers that never mix roles. A tall, high-contrast serif carries the few statements that
matter, set large and sparingly, like an engraved plate. A restrained grotesque handles labels and short explanations. A
monospaced face records numbers, units and reference marks, as an instrument would print them. Text is always scarce; it
anchors what the drawing already shows and never explains at length. Every line sits on a shared baseline grid, and
nothing touches, crowds or overlaps anything else, which is the mark of master-level execution.

Data is drawn, not charted. Paths are thin lines with real geometry; quantities become bars and dots whose positions can
be read with a ruler; repetition (many seeds, many layouts) is shown by accumulation of marks rather than by summarising
them away. Hairline rules, small numbered reference markers and registration ticks give each page the authority of a plate
from a technical atlas.

Rhythm across pages comes from a fixed frame that each page fills differently: a statement page, a mechanism page, a
measurement page, a ledger page. The sequence should feel like a carefully bound set of plates in which every alignment
has been refined many times, quiet enough to study at length and precise enough to be believed.
